How PSA Grading Works: Every Step From Submission to Slab

Updated November 9, 2026 · Gefjon Studio

  3. How the number is decided

How PSA grading works, in one breath: you build a submission online or in the PSA app, ship your cards, and PSA logs them under an order number, checks that each card is genuine, grades its condition from 1 to 10, seals it in a tamper-evident holder with a label, runs two more quality checks and sends it back. The grade comes from four things: centering, corners, edges and surface. Every step is the same for every card, so the only part you control is which cards you send.

This guide walks through each stage in order, explains what the grade, the qualifiers and the "no grade" codes mean, and shows 5 worked examples of how a card can go wrong before it ever reaches a grader.

Quick answer

  • Who decides: at least 2 PSA graders examine every card, according to PSA's FAQ.
  • What they check: authenticity first, then centering, corners, edges and surface.
  • What you get: a grade from 1 to 10 (Gem Mint 10 is the top), a label with a certification number and a sealed holder.
  • What you pay: a per-card fee for the service level you pick. PSA says you're still charged if an item fails its process, with a few exceptions covered below.
  • What you can test at home: centering. It's the only flaw you can measure to a number before you send anything.

How PSA grading works, step by step

1. Choose your cards

Pick the cards that could realistically earn a high grade and sell for a lot more graded than raw. Our guide to whether your card is worth grading has the 5-step price check. Sending 10 cards you've screened beats sending 40 you haven't.

2. Build the submission

PSA's own overview calls this step 2: you build the submission online or in the PSA app. You list each card, state a declared value and choose a service level. Service levels trade price for speed and are tied to the value of the card, and PSA changes both, so read the current price list on the day you submit. Don't trust a number from a forum post.

3. Prepare and ship

Put each card in a penny sleeve, then a semi-rigid holder, and use a rigid box with padding. PSA publishes a packaging guide, so follow it. Insure the package. Plan on 15 minutes to sleeve and pack 10 cards properly.

4. Arrival and order entry

Your package arrives at PSA's facility in Southern California, where the submission label on the side is scanned. Your order is entered into the grading system and assigned an order number that replaces your personal details. PSA says this removes the chance of bias, so the grader never sees whose card it is.

5. Stickers and research

Each card gets a sticker with a unique certification number. PSA's research department identifies the card (year, set, number, variety) so the label is correct. This is why a wrong card description on your form can cost you a label correction later. PSA says it fixes mislabelled items free.

6. Authentication

Grading can only happen after a card is judged authentic. Graders look for counterfeits and for signs of doctoring, such as trimming, recolouring or cleaning.

7. Grading

If the card passes, graders assess condition on the 1 to 10 scale. A Gem Mint 10 is described as a virtually perfect card: four sharp corners, sharp focus, full original gloss and no staining. Centering for a 10 must not exceed approximately 55/45 on the front and 75/25 on the back. PSA's FAQ says the second grader's review is now folded into this stage.

8. Label, seal and checks

A label printed with the card's details and grade goes into the holder, and the card is sonically sealed. PSA then runs a grade check by another grader and a final quality check. According to its FAQ, the quality check looks at the holder's condition, and payment is requested when grading finishes.

9. Reveal and return

You see the grades online or in the PSA app. From there you can have the cards shipped home, store them in PSA's vault or list them for sale through PSA. The window for choosing a destination closes when the order moves to Complete.

How the number is decided

PSA's published standards use these approximate centering limits on the front. Back limits are looser, and PSA reserves the right to make a judgment call based on eye appeal.

  • PSA 10 (Gem Mint): about 55/45 front, 75/25 back
  • PSA 9 (Mint): about 60/40 front, 90/10 back
  • PSA 8 (Near Mint-Mint): about 65/35 front

Centering is read as the percentage split of the borders at the worst point of the card, left to right and top to bottom. If you want the formula and a method for measuring it, read how to measure card centering.

Qualifiers

A card can earn a number plus a qualifier when one flaw falls below the standard for that grade:

  • OC off centre
  • ST staining
  • PD print defect
  • OF out of focus
  • MK marks, such as pen or pencil
  • MC miscut

"No grade" codes

Some cards come back without a number. PSA's list includes N1 (evidence of trimming), N2 (restoration), N3 (recolouring), N4 (questionable authenticity), N5 (altered stock), N7 (cleaning), N6 (undersized), N8 (miscut) and N9 (don't grade). For N6, N8 and N9, PSA says you won't be charged the grading fee. For the tampering codes, the fee still applies.

5 worked examples

Fees below use a round $30 all-in per card for grading, shipping and insurance. That's an illustration, not a PSA quote.

Example 1: The 57/43 front

Left border 2.2 mm, right 1.7 mm. 2.2 ÷ 3.9 = 56.4%, so 56/44. That's outside the approximate 55/45 limit for a 10 and inside 60/40 for a 9. If the card only pays off as a 10, you've found out for free that it won't. Expected best case: a 9.

Example 2: Perfect front, 80/20 back

Front is 52/48 both ways. Back left 3.2 mm, right 0.8 mm: 3.2 ÷ 4.0 = 80/20. That's past 75/25, so the back alone rules out a 10. Always measure both sides.

Example 3: Screening a batch

You own 20 cards and each costs $30 to send, so $600 for all of them. At home you measure all 20 and find 6 with a front worse than 60/40. Dropping those 6 saves $180. Ten minutes of measuring per 10 cards is cheap next to that.

Example 4: The "fixed" corner

A card has a slightly rough edge, and the owner trims 0.5 mm to tidy it. A grader sees a hooked, uneven edge and returns it as N1, evidence of trimming. The $30 is gone, the card has no grade, and it's worth less than before. Don't touch edges.

Example 5: The factory miscut

A card arrives from the pack with the image shifted so far that part of the next card shows. That's a genuine factory miscut, and under PSA's list it may be coded N8, with no grading fee. A miscut is not the same as being off centre: an off-centre card is simply graded lower, or tagged OC.

What to do before you submit

  1. Measure centering on the front and back of every candidate. In Miscut that's about 1 minute per card.
  2. Inspect corners and surface under a bright light, tilted. Be harsh.
  3. Check the price gap using sold listings, not asking prices.
  4. Count the full cost per card, then the total.
  5. Keep a photo log of the front and back of each card, so you can compare the grade you get with your own estimate.

FAQ

Does PSA charge you if your card doesn't get a grade?

Often yes. PSA says that if an item fails its process you're still responsible for the charges. The exceptions it lists are N6 (undersized), N8 (miscut) and N9 (don't grade).

Do you get feedback on why you got a grade?

Sometimes. PSA's Grader Notes show the flaws found in corners, edges, surface and centering once your grades are ready. They aren't included for PSA 10s, for no-grades or for ineligible service levels.

Can I send a card back for a second look?

PSA has review and reholder services. A review isn't a guarantee: the card can come back with the same grade, and fees apply. Read the current terms before you pay.

How long does grading take?

It varies a lot by service level and by how busy PSA is. Check the estimate shown when you submit and track the order in your account. Don't plan a sale around an old number.
