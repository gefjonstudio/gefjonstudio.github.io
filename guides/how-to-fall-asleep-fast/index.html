How to Fall Asleep Fast: 10 Methods, Ranked by What Actually Works

If you want to know how to fall asleep fast, the honest answer is that no trick knocks everyone out in 60 seconds. What works is a short list of things that calm your body, quiet your mind and remove whatever keeps waking you up. Below are 10 methods, what the research says about each, and a simple plan you can follow tonight.

Most healthy adults take somewhere around 10 to 20 minutes to fall asleep. If it regularly takes you more than 30 minutes, three or more nights a week, that's worth mentioning to a doctor, because insomnia is very treatable.

The quick answer

  • Tonight: cool, dark room, phone face down, steady background sound, then 4 rounds of 4-7-8 breathing.
  • If your mind is racing: try the cognitive shuffle (method 3) instead of counting sheep.
  • If you're still awake after about 20 minutes: get up, do something dull in dim light, and go back when you feel sleepy.
  • For the long run: the same wake-up time every day, morning daylight and less caffeine after lunch.

1. Slow your breathing with 4-7-8

Breathe in through your nose for 4 counts, hold for 7, then breathe out slowly through your mouth for 8. Repeat four times.

What the evidence says: there's no large trial of 4-7-8 breathing for sleep. A small 2022 study found it lowered heart rate and blood pressure in young adults, and slow breathing in general improved self-reported sleep in most small studies. It's free, takes a minute and has no downside, so it's worth trying first.

Tip: the long breath out is the important part. If holding for 7 feels uncomfortable, use 4-4-6 and keep the exhale longer than the inhale.

2. The "military method" (body scan)

Relax your face, including your jaw and tongue. Drop your shoulders and let your arms go heavy. Breathe out and relax your chest, then your legs from thighs to feet. Then picture a calm scene for 10 seconds, such as lying in a canoe on a still lake.

What the evidence says: the famous "96% of pilots asleep in 2 minutes" figure comes from a 1981 sports book, Relax and Win, not a clinical trial. The method itself is a version of progressive muscle relaxation, which is a well-known relaxation technique. Treat it as a helpful routine, not a guarantee.

3. The cognitive shuffle (for a racing mind)

Pick a random word like "blanket". For each letter, think of unrelated words and picture each one for a few seconds: B for banana, bicycle, bridge; L for lemon, lighthouse. When you get bored of a letter, move to the next.

Why it helps: worrying keeps your brain in problem-solving mode. Picturing random, harmless images mimics the jumbled thoughts you've as you drift off. Research so far is limited to small studies, but many people with busy minds find it works better than counting.

4. Mask the noises that wake you

Sudden sounds such as a car door, a partner snoring or a neighbour's footsteps are what jolt you awake, not steady sound. A constant sound like rain, a fan or brown noise makes those spikes less noticeable.

There are a few kinds to try:

  • White noise: every frequency at equal level. Bright and hissy, best at covering voices.
  • Pink noise: softer at the top, like steady rain. Many people find it gentler for a whole night.
  • Brown noise: deep and rumbly, like a waterfall or plane cabin. Popular with light sleepers and people with tinnitus.
  • Nature sounds: rain, ocean, wind, a crackling fire. Less clinical if pure noise feels harsh.

What the evidence says: results for continuous noise are mixed, so test it yourself for a week. Keep the volume low (well under the level of normal conversation) and use a timer if you only need help falling asleep.

5. Make the room cool, dark and boring

  • Temperature: most people sleep best somewhere around 60 to 67°F (16 to 19°C). Your body temperature needs to drop slightly to fall asleep.
  • Light: blackout curtains or an eye mask. Cover standby lights and turn the clock away.
  • Bed is for sleep: no work, scrolling or arguments in bed, so your brain links it with sleep.

6. Get up after about 20 minutes

Lying in bed frustrated teaches your brain that bed is a place for being awake. If you've been awake for what feels like 20 minutes, get up, sit somewhere dim and do something dull (a paper book, folding laundry), then go back when you feel sleepy. This is part of "stimulus control", one of the best-supported methods in insomnia treatment.

7. Try paradoxical intention

Lie in bed with your eyes open and gently try to stay awake. It sounds backwards, but trying hard to sleep creates pressure that keeps you up. Taking that pressure away often lets sleep arrive on its own.

8. Write tomorrow's to-do list

Spend five minutes before bed writing down what you need to do tomorrow. A small Baylor University study found people who wrote a specific to-do list fell asleep faster than people who wrote about things they had already done. Getting the list out of your head means your brain stops rehearsing it.

9. Watch caffeine, alcohol and late meals

  • Caffeine: it stays in your system for hours. Try none after about 1 or 2 pm.
  • Alcohol: it can make you drowsy, but it breaks up sleep later in the night, which is why you might wake at 3 am.
  • Big meals: finish heavy food two to three hours before bed.

10. Fix the timing, not just the night

The biggest long-term lever is your body clock. Get up at the same time every day, weekends included, and get daylight in the first hour after waking. After a few days, your sleepiness starts arriving at a regular time in the evening, and falling asleep gets easier without any tricks.

A 20-minute plan for tonight

  1. 30 minutes before bed: dim the lights, put your phone on charge across the room.
  2. In bed: start a steady sound at low volume with a 30-minute fade-out timer.
  3. First 2 minutes: 4 rounds of 4-7-8 breathing.
  4. Next few minutes: body scan from face to feet.
  5. If thoughts keep coming: cognitive shuffle with a random word.
  6. Still awake after about 20 minutes: get up, dim light, something dull, back to bed when sleepy.

When to talk to a doctor

See a doctor if poor sleep lasts more than a few weeks, if you snore loudly or stop breathing in your sleep, or if you feel very sleepy during the day. The most effective long-term treatment for insomnia is a short course of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which many clinics and apps offer.

Frequently asked questions

How can I fall asleep in 5 minutes?

Combine a cool, dark room, a steady background sound and 4 rounds of 4-7-8 breathing. Many people fall asleep within a few minutes this way, but if it takes longer, that's normal.

Is it better to sleep with white noise or brown noise?

It depends on what keeps you awake. White noise is better at covering voices; brown noise is deeper and many people find it more relaxing. Try each for a few nights at low volume.

Why can't I fall asleep even when I'm tired?

Stress, caffeine late in the day, an irregular schedule and light from screens are the most common reasons. Being tired but "wired" usually means your mind is still in problem-solving mode, which is where the cognitive shuffle and a to-do list help.
