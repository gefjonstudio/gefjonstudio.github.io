To translate without internet on an iPhone, download the languages you need while you're on Wi-Fi, switch translation to on-device, then test it once in Airplane Mode before you leave. After that, typed text, phrases and in-person conversations keep working with no signal at all. The part that goes wrong is the setup, not the translating.

Most guides stop at "download your language pack". They skip what happens when you land in a station with no signal and the app asks for a download. This guide covers the setup, a 5-minute test, and the 4 things that can still break.

Quick answer

  1. Connect to Wi-Fi and have about 1 GB free.
  2. Download each language you need, in both directions.
  3. Turn on on-device translation so nothing waits for a server.
  4. Turn on Airplane Mode and Wi-Fi off, then translate 3 test sentences.
  5. Save your 10 most important phrases and your hotel address.
  6. Keep the camera and voice tests separate, because they fail separately.
  7. Charge up and carry a plan B (a screenshot beats a dead signal).

Why a download is needed at all

A translation app has two ways to work. It can send your text to a server and get the answer back, which needs a signal. Or it can run a language model on the phone itself, which doesn't. The second way needs the model on the phone first. That's all a "language pack" is: a file you download once so the phone can translate by itself.

Because those models have to fit on a phone, they're generally smaller than the ones on a server. In practice offline results are good for everyday sentences and weaker on slang, idioms and long, complicated sentences. Keep your sentences short and the gap mostly disappears.

Step 1: Do the downloads on Wi-Fi, a week before

Don't leave this for the airport. Hotel and airport Wi-Fi is slow, and a pack that stalls at 80 percent is useless.

Worked example: you're going to Japan for 10 days and you speak English. You need English and Japanese. If you plan to read Korean signs on a day trip to Seoul, add Korean. That's 3 downloads, done on your home Wi-Fi 7 days before the flight, with a spare day to fix problems.

On a recent iPhone, the built-in Translate app keeps its packs here:

  • iOS 26 and later: Settings, then Apps, then Translate, then Languages. Tap the download button next to each one.
  • Older iOS versions: Settings, then Translate, then Downloaded Languages.

If a language doesn't show a download button, your iPhone or iOS version doesn't support it offline. Better to learn that at home than at the gate. Availability differs between devices, so check your own phone and don't trust a list written for someone else's.

Step 2: Download both directions

You'll translate both ways: your English into Japanese, and the reply or the sign back into English. So you need your own language and the destination language on the phone. Open the app, set the pair to each direction you'll use, and make sure both languages show as downloaded, not as "Get". If you copy Japanese text from a sign or a booking email, try pasting it too.

Step 3: Switch on on-device translation

This is the setting people miss. Apple's own help page describes two options for on-device translation:

  • Automatic: the phone translates on-device when the languages are downloaded, and may use Apple's servers when they're not.
  • Always: translations are always processed on your phone, using your downloaded languages.

For travel, "Always" removes the surprise. If something isn't downloaded, you find out immediately instead of getting a mysterious delay. It's also the privacy-friendly choice: your text stays on the iPhone.

Step 4: The 5-minute Airplane Mode test

Do this at home, the evening after downloading.

  1. Turn on Airplane Mode. Check that Wi-Fi is off too, since it's easy to turn back on without noticing.
  2. Type 3 sentences and read the results: "Where is the nearest pharmacy?", "I'm allergic to peanuts.", "How much is this?"
  3. Swap the direction and translate a sentence back.
  4. Paste a longer paragraph of about 50 words and see whether it finishes.
  5. Close the app fully, open it again and check the languages still show as downloaded.

If all 5 pass, your text translation is ready. If step 4 or 5 fails, delete the pack, restart the phone and download it again on Wi-Fi.

Step 5: Know the 4 things that can still need a signal

"Works offline" is often true for text and not true for everything else. Test each of these separately.

  1. Voice input. Speech recognition is a separate model from translation. On some setups it needs its own language download, and it can be less accurate in a loud street. Say a sentence in Airplane Mode and check it was understood.
  2. Spoken playback. The voice that reads the result aloud may be a different download from the text pack. Check your phone's speech settings if playback is silent or robotic.
  3. Camera translation. It has to read the text before translating it, and some scripts (vertical Japanese is a good example) are harder than horizontal text. Photograph a sign at home and try it offline.
  4. Languages with no offline pack. A few languages only work online. Check the list before you pick a destination plan.

Worked example: you're hiking in the Alps with a hut booked in German, and there's no signal above the tree line. Text you paste in translates fine, because that pack is on the phone. Before you left, the Airplane Mode test told you whether voice input and spoken playback worked too. If either failed, you saved the 5 phrases you needed most as text and screenshots and carried on.

Step 6: Save your top 10 phrases

Even a perfect offline translator is slow when you're tired, in the rain, or in a queue. For the situations that matter, don't translate live. Save the result ahead of time.

A good list of 10 for any trip:

  • "I need a doctor." / "Where is the nearest hospital?"
  • "I'm allergic to [food]." (name it in the local language)
  • "I'm lost. Can you show me on a map?"
  • "Does this have meat / dairy / nuts?"
  • "How much is this?"
  • "Please call me a taxi to [address]."
  • "I don't understand. Please write it down."
  • "Where is the toilet?"
  • "The bill, please."
  • Your hotel name and address in the local script

Worked example: you land in Tokyo at 22:40 and your hotel is 35 minutes from the airport. Save the hotel name and address in Japanese now. A taxi driver can read it off your screen in 5 seconds, and it works without a signal, a charge in your pocket or a language pack.

Step 7: Prepare for the dead-phone and slow-phone cases

  • Battery: the camera and microphone drain faster than text. Carry a small power bank (10,000 mAh is roughly 2 to 3 phone charges).
  • Screenshots: screenshot your hotel address, your booking and your 10 saved phrases. A screenshot opens in 1 second and never needs a download.
  • Low Power Mode: it can slow background downloads, so finish downloads before you turn it on.
  • Storage: if your iPhone is nearly full, downloads fail quietly. Free up about 1 GB first.
  • Updates: an iOS update can change which packs are installed. Re-run the 5-minute test after any update on the trip.

How long each step takes

  • Downloads: a few minutes to about 15 per language on home Wi-Fi, depending on the language and your connection.
  • Settings and on-device switch: 2 minutes.
  • Airplane Mode test: 5 minutes.
  • Saving 10 phrases and screenshots: 10 minutes.
  • Total: about 30 minutes, once.

When translation apps aren't enough

Machine translation is a guide, not a guarantee. For medical information, legal documents and allergies, use the translation to start the conversation and confirm with a person, a printed card or an official interpreter. If you have a severe allergy, carry a card written by a native speaker. That card works when the battery doesn't.

Frequently asked questions

Can you translate without internet on iPhone?

Yes. Download the languages on Wi-Fi first, then text translation works with no signal. Test it once in Airplane Mode before you travel.

Does translating offline use mobile data?

No. Once the language is on the phone, an on-device translation doesn't send anything. Only the first download uses data, so do it on Wi-Fi.

Is offline translation as accurate as online?

Usually a bit less on slang and long sentences, and about the same for short, everyday phrases. Keep sentences under about 15 words for the best results.

Can two people have a conversation through one phone offline?

Yes, as long as both languages are downloaded. Apple's help page says conversations work for downloaded languages when you don't have an internet connection. Put the phone between you or pass it back and forth.
