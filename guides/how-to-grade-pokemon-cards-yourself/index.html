How to Grade Pokemon Cards Yourself: A 4-Point Home Check

You can't grade Pokemon cards yourself in a way the market will accept. A slab from PSA, BGS, CGC or SGC is what buyers pay for. But you can pre-grade a card at home in about 3 minutes and predict the likely range, and that's what stops you paying a $30 to $50 fee on a card that was never going to score well.

This guide gives you a 4-point check (centering, corners, edges, surface), a simple scoring rule where the weakest point sets the ceiling, and five scored examples with real numbers.

Quick answer

  1. Measure the centering on the front and back. This is the only point you can calculate exactly.
  2. Check all four corners with a 10x loupe.
  3. Check all four edges, front and back, under angled light.
  4. Check the surface by tilting the card under one bright lamp.
  5. Give each point a grade range. The lowest range is your card's ceiling.
  6. Subtract one from that ceiling before you decide anything.

Why a home grade is only an estimate

A professional grader sees the card under controlled light, with training and a lot of cards to compare against. You see one card, once, and you want it to be good. Both of those bias the result upward.

Two honest limits:

  • Companies publish their centering limits as numbers, but corners, edges and surface are judged by eye. Your 10x loupe finds the same flaws, but you can't know where a grader draws the line.
  • Different companies weigh things differently. A card that looks like a 9 at one company can come back differently at another.

So treat your result as a range, not a verdict. Its job is to sort a stack of 50 cards into "worth a closer look" and "leave it in the binder."

What you need

Three cheap tools cover almost everything:

  • A 10x jeweler's loupe, roughly $10. Phone zoom works for centering, but a loupe shows corner and edge whitening that a phone hides.
  • One bright LED lamp, roughly $15. A single hard light source beats a bright room, because flaws show up as reflections when you tilt the card.
  • A dark, soft mat or a microfibre cloth to work on.

Optional: digital calipers (about $10) for measuring borders in millimetres. Wash and dry your hands first and hold cards by the edges. Don't clean or wipe a card to improve it. Grading companies say cleaning or altering a card can get it returned ungraded.

The 4-point check

Point 1: Centering

Centering compares the border on one side with the border on the opposite side. It's written as two numbers that add up to 100, like 55/45.

Centering = wider border ÷ (left border + right border) × 100

Left border 2.3 mm and right border 1.9 mm: total 4.2 mm, 2.3 ÷ 4.2 = 54.8%, so the card is about 55/45 left to right. Do the same for top and bottom, then flip the card and measure the back. The worse of the two numbers on each face is the one that counts.

PSA's published front limits are approximately 55/45 for a 10, 60/40 for a 9, 65/35 for an 8 and 70/30 for a 7. For the back, a 10 allows approximately 75/25 and a 9 allows 90/10. Other graders publish slightly different numbers, and Beckett is stricter. Our guide to how to measure card centering lists every grader's limits and six worked measurements.

Point 2: Corners

Hold the card flat under the lamp and look at each corner through the loupe. You're looking for:

  • White dots or fuzz where the blue back or coloured border has worn away
  • Soft, rounded tips instead of sharp ones
  • Tiny layers lifting apart (called fraying)

Check the front corners, then flip the card and check the back. Front corners can look perfect while the back shows white.

Point 3: Edges

Tilt the card so the lamp skims along each edge at a low angle. Dark borders and Pokemon's blue back show chipping first. Look for:

  • Small white flecks along the edge
  • Rough or uneven cuts from the factory
  • Nicks from sleeves or binders

Some older print runs have rough edges straight from the factory, so a card can be handled carefully and still have this flaw.

Point 4: Surface

This is the hardest point and the one most people skip. Hold the card at 30 to 45 degrees under the lamp, then rotate it slowly, as if you were reading a reflection. You're looking for:

  • Scratches, especially across holo areas
  • Print lines (thin straight lines that run through the foil)
  • Dents, pressure marks and fingerprints
  • Clouding or loss of gloss on the card face

Check the back as well. A scratch you can feel with a fingernail is a serious flaw.

How to score it

Give each of the four points a range. This is our own rule of thumb, not an official grading scale:

  • 10 range: nothing visible under 10x, or centering inside PSA's 10 limit
  • 9 range: one tiny flaw, or centering inside the 9 limit
  • 8 range: two or three tiny flaws, or centering inside the 8 limit
  • 7 or lower: visible whitening, a dent, a print line or a crease

Then take the lowest range. A card is only as strong as its weakest point. A card with a 10-range surface and a 9-range centering has a ceiling of 9. After that, subtract one as a safety margin. Collectors commonly do this because home graders tend to be generous, though that's a rule of thumb, not a measured fact.

5 scored examples

These are made-up cards with measurements chosen to show the method.

Example 1: The clean modern holo

Front centering: left 2.2 mm, right 2.0 mm gives 52/48. Top 2.8 mm, bottom 2.4 mm gives 54/46. Back is 58/42. Corners: three sharp, one with a tiny white dot at the bottom right. Edges clean. Surface clean.

Scores: centering 10, corners 9, edges 10, surface 10. The ceiling is 9 because of one corner dot. After the safety subtract, expect an 8 to 9.

Example 2: The vintage card with a great face

Surface and edges look wonderful. Corners are sharp. But front centering is left 3.5 mm, right 1.5 mm: 3.5 ÷ 5.0 = 70%, so 70/30.

Scores: centering 7, everything else 9 or 10. The ceiling is 7. No amount of clean surface fixes a cut that sits to one side.

Example 3: Perfect front, whitened back

Front centering is 52/48 both ways. Corners are sharp. Under the lamp, the back edge shows white flecks in three places along the top.

Scores: centering 10, corners 10, edges 8, surface 10. The ceiling is 8. This is why you check the back before you get excited about the front.

Example 4: The hidden print line

Under room light the holo looks flawless. Under the lamp at a shallow angle, one thin straight line runs across the foil.

Scores: centering 10, corners 10, edges 10, surface 8 or lower. The ceiling is 8. Print lines are a common reason holo cards miss the top grades, and they only show when you tilt the card.

Example 5: The real candidate

Front centering 51/49 and 53/47. Back 60/40. All four corners sharp under 10x. Edges clean front and back. Surface clean under two lamp angles.

Scores: 10, 10, 10, 10. The ceiling is 10, and after the safety subtract you plan for a 9. Only now does it make sense to compare the fee with what a 9 and a 10 sell for. Our guide to whether your card is worth grading walks through that maths.

A 20-card sample session

Sorting a stack goes quicker when you split the work:

  1. Minutes 0 to 10: Photograph or measure the centering of all 20 cards, front only. Anything worse than 60/40 goes in the "pass" pile. That usually removes a good share of the stack with no loupe work.
  2. Minutes 10 to 40: Take the survivors under the loupe and the lamp. Check corners, then edges, then surface. Note any flaw on a sticky label, not on the card.
  3. Minutes 40 to 50: Measure the back centering for the cards still standing.
  4. Minutes 50 to 60: Rank what's left by ceiling, highest first.

At around 3 minutes a card for the full check, an hour covers 20 cards, and the centering pass at the start saves you the most time.

Common mistakes

  • Judging in the toploader. The plastic scratches and reflects. Take the card out of the sleeve.
  • Using room light. One hard lamp shows what a ceiling light hides.
  • Skipping the back. Back edges and corners count.
  • Averaging. A grade isn't an average of the four points. The weakest point caps it.
  • Rounding in your favour. 55.6/44.4 isn't 55/45.
  • Cleaning the card first. Don't. It can do more harm than the flaw.

When to stop and just send it

If you have one card you love, a $40 fee may be worth paying for the certainty. Home grading is for deciding between cards, not for second-guessing a card you'll keep.

Frequently asked questions

Can you grade your own Pokemon cards?

You can inspect and estimate a grade, but only a grading company's slab is recognised by buyers and marketplaces. A home grade is a pre-grade that helps you decide what to submit.

What's the easiest flaw to measure at home?

Centering. It's a ratio of border widths, so you can calculate it exactly from a ruler, a scan or an app. Corners, edges and surface need a loupe and a judgement call.

Does a home grade of 10 mean I'll get a PSA 10?

No. Treat it as a ceiling, not a promise. Plan for one grade lower, and remember that only the grading company's grade counts.

Do I need an app to pre-grade?
