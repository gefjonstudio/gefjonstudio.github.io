If you're wondering how to sleep with noise around you, the honest answer is that you rarely win by making a room silent. You win by shrinking the noise that gets in, then blurring what's left so it stops sounding like an event. Seal the gaps, put distance and soft things between you and the source, and add a steady sound that covers the sudden bangs. Most people notice a difference within 3 to 7 nights.

Below are 8 fixes in the order I'd try them, with worked numbers for traffic, upstairs neighbours and thin walls, plus what the evidence does and doesn't show.

The quick answer

  • Tonight, free: close the window, roll a towel along the bottom of the door, and play a steady sound (brown noise, a fan or rain) at about 40 to 45 decibels at your pillow.
  • This week: move the bed away from the noisy wall and add soft mass: a rug, thick curtains, a bookcase.
  • If it still wakes you: try soft foam earplugs, and test each fix for 3 to 7 nights before judging it.
  • See a doctor if you're tired all day even after 7 to 8 hours in bed, or you wake gasping.

Why noise wakes you even when you "sleep through it"

Your brain keeps listening while you sleep. What pulls you out of deep sleep isn't average loudness, it's a sudden change from what came before.

The World Health Organization suggests bedroom noise stays under about 30 decibels over the night, with single events kept under about 45 decibels, to protect sleep. For scale, a quiet bedroom sits near 30 and a normal conversation is around 60.

Worked example: a bedroom at 30 decibels, and a motorbike passes outside at 70, with the window closed cutting around 25. That's 45 reaching you, a 15 decibel jump. With the window open, only about 10 to 15 is cut, so 55 to 60 reaches you, a jump of 25 to 30. Same bike, but the jump is almost double. That's why a closed window matters more than any gadget.

1. Work out what kind of noise you've got

Different noise needs a different fix. Spend one evening listening.

  • Steady and constant (road hum, air con, a fridge): easiest to mask.
  • Sudden and irregular (door slams, dogs, sirens): the jump is the problem, so masking plus sealing helps most.
  • Low and thumping (bass through a wall, upstairs footsteps): travels through structure, hard to block, best handled with distance, soft mass and a deep masking sound.

2. Close the gaps first

Sound leaks through gaps far more than through solid walls. Fix the gaps before you buy anything.

  • Window: shut it if you can ventilate another way. A closed single-glazed window can cut roughly 25 decibels, an open one about 10 to 15.
  • Door: a rolled towel or a draught excluder along the bottom blocks a surprising amount of corridor and landing noise.

Worked example: your flat has a 1 cm gap under the bedroom door, and the TV in the next room is 55 decibels. A rolled towel might take 5 to 10 off that. Small number, but it moves the sound from "words I can follow" toward "murmur", which is the bit that keeps your brain interested.

3. Move the bed

Sound drops with distance, and walls shared with neighbours carry the most.

  1. Pull the bed 30 to 50 cm (1 to 1.5 feet) away from a shared wall.
  2. If you can, put your head at the end of the room farthest from the road or lift shaft.

4. Add soft mass

Hard surfaces bounce sound around. A thick rug, lined curtains, and a full bookcase on the shared wall soak it up. None is dramatic alone, but together they turn a sharp, echoing sound into a duller one.

5. Cover the leftover noise with a steady sound

This is where a masking sound earns its place. You aren't trying to out-shout the noise. You're raising the quiet baseline so the sudden bangs stand out less.

  • Brown noise: deep, like a distant waterfall. Good for low rumbles and bass.
  • Pink noise: softer than white, like steady rain. A good all-rounder.
  • White noise: bright and hissy. It covers high sounds like voices, but some people find it harsh.
  • Fan or air conditioner: steady and cheap, and many people already own one.
  • Steady rain or ocean waves: pleasant, but pick a loop with no thunder or crashing.

Worked example: the room is 30 decibels and a neighbour's door slams at 60, a 30 decibel jump. Play brown noise at 42 and the room's floor is now 42, so the slam is an 18 decibel jump. Same slam, a much gentler contrast, and much less likely to wake you.

Setup tips:

  1. Put the speaker between you and the noise source, such as near the window or shared wall.
  2. Start quiet and raise it in small steps until the bangs stop poking through, then stop. Keep it around 40 to 50 decibels at your pillow, roughly light rain.
  3. Use a timer that lasts all night. A sound that cuts out at 1am leaves you alone with the 3am noises.

6. Try earplugs, and fit them properly

Soft foam earplugs rated 29 to 33 decibels of reduction are the strongest low-tech option. Real-world results are lower than the rating, often by 5 to 10 decibels, because the fit is never perfect.

  1. Roll the plug thin between your fingers.
  2. Pull your ear up and back, and insert it.
  3. Hold it in place for 20 to 30 seconds while it expands.

Worked example: a 60 decibel shout in the street, minus a realistic 20 from earplugs, is about 40 reaching you. Combine that with a closed window (another 25) and the shout is down to around 15, below the 30 decibel level of a quiet bedroom.

Earplugs can make your own breathing and heartbeat louder, and some people can't sleep with anything in their ears. If that's you, stay with fixes 2 to 5.

7. Train your reaction to the noise

Your response to a sound matters as much as the sound. If you lie there thinking "there it is again", the sound becomes a threat and your body stays alert.

  • Don't clock-watch. Turn the clock away. Counting how many hours are left makes it worse.
  • Breathe slowly. Breathe in for 4 counts, out for 6, for 2 to 3 minutes.
  • Give your mind something to follow. A steady sound, or a boring story you've heard before.

Many people do get used to a steady noise over weeks, though research is mixed on how much it protects sleep depth, which is why you still want to cut the noise itself. Our guide on how to fall asleep fast has 10 more wind-down methods ranked by evidence.

8. Handle the specific situation

Three common ones, with what I'd do first.

  • Noisy upstairs neighbours: footsteps are low and travel through the ceiling, so earplugs help less than you'd hope. Use brown noise, move your bed away from the ceiling area that's under their living room, and send a polite message with specific times ("it's been about 1am to 2am on weeknights"). A calm note works more often than people expect.
  • Traffic or a main road: use fix 2 (shut the window) first, add a steady masking sound pointed at the window, and move your head to the far end of the room.
  • Thin walls and a snoring partner next door or beside you: masking and earplugs together help most. Our guide on how to block out snoring goes further.

If you wake at 3am and can't get back to sleep, see how to stop waking up at 3am.

What the evidence does and doesn't show

  • Noise harms sleep: the link between night noise and disturbed sleep is well supported, which is why the WHO publishes bedroom limits.
  • Masking sound: studies are small and mixed. Some find steady sound reduces how often noise wakes people, others find little change, and a quiet bedroom is still best when you can get one. Treat masking as something to test on yourself for 7 nights.

A 7-night test

Do fixes 2 and 3 on nights 1 and 2, add masking at 40 to 45 decibels on nights 3 to 5, and add earplugs on nights 6 and 7 if you still wake. Write down one number each morning: how many times you woke. If it falls from 4 to 2, that's progress.

When to see a doctor

Noise tips are fine for ordinary light sleepers. Talk to a doctor if you're sleepy all day despite 7 to 8 hours in bed, if you wake gasping or choking, or if you hear ringing or buzzing that doesn't stop when it's quiet. If the ringing is the main issue, read how to sleep with tinnitus.

FAQ

How do I sleep with noise around me?

Close the gaps around your window and door, move your bed away from the noisy wall, and add a steady masking sound such as brown noise or a fan at around 40 to 45 decibels. Add soft foam earplugs if you still wake.

Can you get used to sleeping with noise?

Many people adapt to steady, constant noise over a few weeks. Sudden, irregular noise like slamming doors or sirens is harder to adapt to, so reduce and mask it instead of waiting.

Is white noise bad for sleeping?

For adults, a quiet steady sound is generally considered fine. Keep the volume low, keep the speaker away from your ear, and stop if you notice ringing or discomfort. It doesn't improve sleep for everyone.

Do noise-cancelling headphones work for sleep?

They help most with steady low hums like engines and air con. They're weaker against voices and sudden bangs, and some people find them uncomfortable when lying on their side.
