How to Sleep With Tinnitus: 7 Fixes for Quieter Nights

If you want to know how to sleep with tinnitus, the short answer is to stop making your bedroom silent. Tinnitus is the ringing, buzzing or hissing you hear with no outside source, and a quiet room is where it sounds loudest. Add a soft, steady sound, keep it a bit lower than the tinnitus itself, and pair it with a calm wind-down. Most people notice a difference within 7 to 14 nights, and the sound doesn't need to be fancy.

Below are 7 fixes in the order I'd try them, plus what the evidence shows and when to see a doctor first.

The quick answer

  • Tonight, free: play a soft fan, pink noise or steady rain across the room, at a level where you can still faintly hear your tinnitus.
  • This week: use the same sound every night and keep a regular bedtime, so your brain starts to treat it as boring.
  • If you wake at 2am: don't lie there fighting it. Keep the sound on, get up after about 25 minutes, do something calm, then try again.
  • See a doctor if the ringing is in one ear only, started suddenly, pulses with your heartbeat or comes with dizziness or hearing loss.

Why tinnitus is louder at night

Tinnitus doesn't get louder at bedtime, you just notice it more. During the day, traffic, voices and the fridge fill your ears. At night the room might sit around 30 decibels, so a quiet internal sound at 20 or 25 decibels has nothing to compete with. Add worry about not sleeping, and your brain keeps checking on it.

Tinnitus UK describes this the same way: the shift from a noisy day to a quiet bedroom can make the noises more noticeable, and they suggest adding low-level sound to the bedroom.

Worked example: say your tinnitus feels like a thin hiss at "3 out of 10" in a quiet room. Add a fan at about 35 decibels and the hiss slips to about "1 out of 10" because the fan now takes up some of your attention. You haven't removed anything. You've given your brain a calmer thing to listen to.

1. Add a steady background sound

This is the one fix every major source agrees on. The Sleep Foundation, WHO, WebMD and Tinnitus UK all suggest a fan, white noise machine or soothing sounds for bedtime. Evidence is modest and mixed, because tinnitus differs so much from person to person, but it's cheap, safe to try and easy to stop if it doesn't help.

What to try:

  • A box fan or air conditioner: steady, familiar and already in your house.
  • Pink noise: like steady rain, softer than white noise. A good first choice.
  • Brown noise: deeper and rumbly. Better if your tinnitus is a low hum.
  • White noise: bright and hissy. It can cover a high whistle, but some people find it harsh at night.
  • Rain, ocean waves or a brook: pleasant, but pick a loop with no thunder, no crashing waves and no sudden bird calls.

Match the pitch. A high-pitched whistle often hides best behind a brighter sound, such as white noise or a rain shower. A low hum hides better behind brown noise or a fan. Try each for 3 nights and note how long you take to fall asleep.

2. Get the volume right

This is where most guides stop short. Louder isn't better.

Audiologists who set up sound therapy often talk about the "mixing point": the level where the sound and your tinnitus start to blend together. Several clinical approaches suggest keeping the sound at or just below that point, so you can still faintly hear your tinnitus. The NHS leaflet from Berkshire Healthcare puts it simply: set the sound slightly lower than your tinnitus and you should hear both at once. The reasoning is that your brain learns to ignore a sound it can still hear, and gets no practice if it's completely covered.

Not everyone agrees. Some approaches use full masking for quick relief, and 1 trial comparing the 2 found no significant difference. Treat this as a starting rule, not a law.

A simple volume routine:

  1. Lie in bed with the lights out and start the sound very low. You should barely hear it.
  2. Raise it one small step at a time until it starts to blend with your tinnitus.
  3. Stop there, or go back down one notch.
  4. Leave it. Don't adjust it again at 1am.

Worked example: Priya has a steady 8 kHz ring. At volume 1 of 10 she hears only the ring. At 3 the sound starts to merge with it. At 6 the ring has vanished and she feels tense when it flickers back. She sets 3 and leaves it for the night. Keep it under 50 decibels, roughly the level of light rain.

3. Keep the sound on all night

A 30 minute timer is a trap for tinnitus. The sound fades out just as you drift off, the room goes quiet again, and the ringing is back when you reach a light stage of sleep. Set it to run all night at the same low level, until about 7am.

Worked example: you fall asleep at 11pm with a 45 minute timer. At 1:30am you surface between sleep cycles, hear only the ringing, and you're awake. With the sound on all night, 1:30am passes unnoticed.

4. Build a wind-down that lowers stress

Tinnitus and stress feed each other. Poor sleep makes you more reactive, and stress makes tinnitus seem louder. Tinnitus UK calls tinnitus an "emotional barometer", and the Sleep Foundation notes that lack of sleep doesn't seem to cause tinnitus but can make it feel worse.

A routine that works for most people:

  1. Stop screens and bright light 45 minutes before bed.
  2. Do 5 minutes of slow breathing. Breathe in for 4 counts, out for 6.
  3. Try a body scan or progressive muscle relaxation. Tense each muscle group for 5 seconds, then let go.
  4. Write down tomorrow's worries.
  5. Turn on your sound before you get into bed, not after you notice the ringing.

5. Check caffeine, alcohol and your evening habits

  • Caffeine: the Sleep Foundation notes late caffeine can interfere with sleep and may make tinnitus worse. Try nothing after 2pm for 7 days and see.
  • Alcohol: it can help you nod off, then breaks up the second half of your night. Tinnitus UK makes the same point.
  • Heavy exercise within 2 to 3 hours of bed (WebMD) can leave you wired.
  • Loud noise: it can worsen tinnitus, so wear ear protection at gigs and around power tools.

6. Don't fight a bad night

Tinnitus UK suggests that if you haven't fallen asleep within about 25 minutes, you get up, do something calm in low light, and go back when you feel sleepy. Keep the sound running while you do it.

Worked example: you wake at 2:15am and notice the tinnitus. Instead of checking the clock every 5 minutes, you move to a chair, read a paper book for 20 minutes in dim light, and return to bed at 2:45am. 7. Ask about proper treatment if it's wearing you down

If tinnitus keeps wrecking your nights, ask your doctor or an audiologist about:

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for tinnitus or for insomnia. It's among the better-supported treatments for how much tinnitus bothers you.
  • Sound therapy guided by an audiologist, including combination hearing aids if you also have hearing loss.
  • Treating the cause, such as earwax, jaw problems or a medication side effect.

What the evidence does and doesn't show

  • Masking and sound enrichment: widely recommended and low risk. Study results vary, and it doesn't cure tinnitus.
  • Volume level: several guidelines say stay at or below the mixing point. Trials comparing levels haven't found a clear winner.
  • Sleep and stress: better sleep and lower stress go with tinnitus feeling less intrusive. That's a link, not proof.

When to see a doctor

Sound and habits are fine for long-standing, steady tinnitus that a doctor has already looked at. Book an appointment promptly if:

  • The ringing is in one ear only, or one side is much louder.
  • It started suddenly, or came with sudden hearing loss. Treat that as urgent.
  • It pulses in time with your heartbeat.
  • You have dizziness, ear pain, discharge or weakness in the face.
  • It's affecting your mood, and you feel low, anxious or hopeless.

FAQ

What is the best sound to sleep with tinnitus?

There isn't one. A fan, pink noise, brown noise or steady rain are the usual starting points. Match the pitch loosely, try each for a few nights, and pick the one that lets you fall asleep fastest.

Should the sound completely cover my tinnitus?

Not necessarily. Many clinical guides suggest setting it at or just below the level where it blends with your tinnitus, so you can still faintly hear the ringing. Total masking is used in some approaches, so if you need it for relief at first, ask your audiologist.

Is it safe to sleep with white noise on all night?

For most adults a quiet sound you can talk over is generally considered fine. Keep it low, with the speaker about 1 metre from your head. If it ever seems to make the ringing worse, stop and see a doctor.

Can tinnitus go away if I sleep better?

Not reliably, but it often bothers you less after a few weeks of regular sleep, steady sound and lower stress.
