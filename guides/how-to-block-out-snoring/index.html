How to Block Out Snoring: 7 Fixes That Work Tonight

If you want to know how to block out snoring, the short answer is this: you won't silence it, but you can make it stop standing out. A snore is a loud, uneven sound landing in a quiet room, and that jump is what pulls you awake. Cover the jump with a steady sound, add soft things around the bed, or seal your ears, and most people get 6 to 8 hours back without anyone moving to the sofa.

Below are 7 fixes in the order I'd try them, with worked examples, plus what the evidence does and doesn't say, and the signs that snoring needs a doctor and not a gadget.

The quick answer

  • Tonight, free: play steady brown noise, fan or rain on a speaker across the room, just loud enough to blur the snore.
  • If that's not enough: add soft furnishings, move the bed off a bare wall, or use foam earplugs.
  • Longer term: ask your partner to try side sleeping and no alcohol before bed.
  • See a doctor if there are gasps, pauses in breathing or heavy daytime sleepiness.

Why snoring wakes you up in the first place

Your brain keeps listening while you sleep. It isn't the average loudness that wakes you, it's the sudden change.

Worked example: a quiet bedroom sits around 30 decibels. Regular snoring is often in the 50 to 60 decibel range, and loud snoring can pass 80. A snore at 60 in a room at 30 is a 30 decibel jump. Add a steady sound at 45 and the same snore is only a 15 decibel jump above the background. Same snore, half the contrast, far less likely to wake you.

That's why "turn it up until you can't hear it" is the wrong plan. You're not trying to out-shout your partner, you're trying to flatten the difference.

1. Mask it with a steady sound

This is the cheapest fix and the one with the most practical support. Mayo Clinic lists background noise such as a white noise machine or a fan near the bed as a way to cope with a partner's snoring.

Not every sound works equally well. Snoring is mostly low and mid pitched, so you want a sound with real weight in those ranges, and no surprises in it.

  • Brown noise: deep and rumbly, like a strong waterfall heard from far away. Great at covering low-pitched rumbling snores.
  • Pink noise: softer than white noise, a bit like steady rain. A good all-rounder.
  • White noise: bright and hissy. It works for some people, but it can sound harsh at night.
  • A box fan or air conditioner: steady, cheap and already in most houses.
  • Steady rain or ocean waves: pleasant, but pick a loop with no thunder or crashing waves.

Worked example: say your partner snores in bursts that peak at 58 decibels. Set a brown noise at about 45 decibels at your pillow, which is roughly the level of a quiet conversation across a room. The peaks now rise only 13 above the sound, and many people stop noticing them within a few nights.

Setting it up:

  1. Put the speaker across the room or on the snorer's side, not under your own pillow. The sound should reach you from the same direction as the snore, so they blend.
  2. Start low, then raise it in small steps until the snore stops poking through. Then stop. Louder isn't better.
  3. Keep it under about 50 decibels, roughly the level of light rain, and never run it through headphones at high volume.
  4. Use a timer that lasts all night, not 30 minutes. A sound that cuts out at 1am leaves you alone with the 3am snores.

2. Try the honest low-tech extras

Masking isn't the only trick, and stacking two small fixes beats leaning on one.

  • Move the bed. A headboard pushed against a bare, hard wall bounces snore sound back at you. Pull the bed 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 inches) away from the wall, or angle it so the snorer's mouth isn't aimed down the bed.
  • Add soft mass at pillow height. An upholstered headboard, heavier curtains, a rug on a bare floor, a bookcase on the shared wall. Each one soaks up a little. Together, they turn a sharp sound into a muffled one.
  • Use a door. If the snorer is in another room, a draught excluder or a rolled towel along the bottom of the door stops sound slipping through the gap.

3. Use earplugs, and use the right kind

Foam earplugs rated around 30 to 33 decibels of noise reduction are the strongest option. On paper, a snore at 60 decibels drops to roughly 30 inside your ear, about as quiet as the room itself. In real life, you usually get less than the rating, because the fit isn't perfect.

Tips that help:

  1. Roll the plug thin, pull your ear up and back, insert it, and hold it for 20 to 30 seconds while it expands.
  2. Choose soft, low-profile foam if you sleep on your side. Hard plastic ones press on the pillow.
  3. Replace foam plugs every few nights, or sooner if they're dirty.

Earplugs can make your own heartbeat sound louder, and some people can't stand them. If so, go back to fix 1.

4. Try sleep earbuds or a soft headband

If you'd rather keep your ear canals clear, a fabric sleep headband with thin flat speakers plays masking sound while you lie on your side. Keep the volume low enough to hear a quiet voice, and check the battery lasts 8 hours. For longer use, a speaker across the room is gentler on your ears.

5. Ask the snorer to try these

Reducing the source helps both of you, and these changes are free.

  • Sleep on the side. Snoring is often worse on the back, when the tongue and soft tissue fall towards the throat. A pillow along the back or a small tennis ball sewn into a pyjama top is the old trick.
  • Skip alcohol close to bedtime. It relaxes the airway muscles. Healthdirect in Australia suggests none for at least 4 hours before sleep.
  • Treat a blocked nose. If snoring comes with a cold or allergy, a saline rinse or the right allergy treatment may help. Nasal strips can open the nose, but they don't fix snoring from the throat.
  • Weight and smoking matter. Extra weight around the neck and smoking both make snoring more likely. Those take time, so pair them with the quick fixes above.

6. Change when you go to sleep

If your partner snores most when they're deepest asleep, getting to sleep before them is often the problem. You're in the loudest part of their night and the lightest part of yours.

Worked example: your partner falls asleep at 10:30pm and the snoring starts within about 20 to 30 minutes. If you can fall asleep with masking noise by 10:30 too, you're already asleep before the first loud snores arrive. If you need longer, start your sound earlier and give yourself a wind-down routine. 7. Plan an exit for the worst nights

Sometimes the best fix is the spare room. Agree it in advance: "After 2 bad nights in a row, I take the spare room, no hard feelings." If you wake at 3am and can't drop back off, see our guide on how to stop waking up at 3am.

What the evidence does and doesn't show

  • Masking noise: results in studies are mixed and many are small. Some find that steady sound reduces how often noise wakes people, others find little change. It's cheap and safe to test, so give it 7 nights.
  • Earplugs: good lab ratings, but fit and comfort decide real-life results.
  • Snoring treatments: side sleeping, cutting alcohol and treating nasal congestion have the best support for ordinary snoring. Medical options exist for stubborn cases.

When to see a doctor

Blocking the sound is fine for ordinary snoring. Don't let it hide something else. Book a visit if the snorer:

  • Stops breathing for a few seconds, gasps or chokes during sleep
  • Is very sleepy during the day, even after 7 to 8 hours in bed
  • Wakes with morning headaches or a dry mouth most days
  • Has high blood pressure, or snoring that suddenly got much worse

Those can be signs of sleep apnoea, which is treatable. Masking sound won't fix it.

FAQ

What is the best sound to block out snoring?

Steady brown or pink noise, a fan or constant rain are good starting points because they have weight in the low and mid pitches where snoring lives. Avoid loops with thunder, music or sudden changes.

How loud should the sound be?

Just loud enough that the snores no longer stand out. For most bedrooms that's around 40 to 50 decibels at your pillow. If you need much more than that, add earplugs or fix the room instead of turning it up.

How can I block out snoring without earplugs?

Combine a steady masking sound across the room with a bed that's away from bare walls, soft furnishings near the head of the bed and a door seal if the snorer is next door. Together these cut and blur the sound.

Is it okay to play noise all night?

For adults, a quiet sound that you can talk over is generally considered fine. Keep it low, keep the speaker away from your ear, and stop if you notice any ringing or discomfort.
