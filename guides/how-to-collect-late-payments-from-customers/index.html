How to Collect Late Payments From Customers: A 7-Step Plan With Messages You Can Copy

To collect a late payment, contact the customer within one to three days of the due date, restate the invoice number, amount and due date, and make paying as easy as one tap. If that fails, follow a fixed schedule: a second message at day 7, a phone call at day 14, a formal demand letter at day 30, and an outside option (collections or small claims court) at day 60 or later. Keep every contact in writing so you have a dated trail.

Most late payers aren't refusing to pay. The invoice got buried, went to the wrong person, or the customer is short on cash this month. Your job is to be the easiest thing in their inbox to deal with, and to be steady rather than angry. This guide gives you the schedule, the exact words to use, and worked examples with numbers.

Quick answer

  1. Day 1 to 3 late: short, friendly reminder with the invoice attached and a way to pay.
  2. Day 7: second reminder, plus a call if it's a large invoice.
  3. Day 14: phone call. Ask for a specific payment date and write it down.
  4. Day 21: email recap of the call, and mention any late fee that's in your agreement.
  5. Day 30: formal demand letter with a final date and what happens next.
  6. Pause new work until the balance is settled.
  7. Day 60 or later: collections agency, a lawyer's letter, or small claims court.

Step 1: Check the basics before you chase

Some "late" invoices turn out to be a mistake on your side. Before you send anything, check three things:

  • Did it reach the right person? Large clients often send invoices to an accounts payable address, not to the person who hired you.
  • Does the invoice have a due date and a number? Without a date, the customer decides when "late" starts.
  • Is the amount correct? A deposit you forgot to subtract is the classic cause of a dispute that looks like non-payment.

If you find a problem, fix it and reissue the invoice with a new due date. It's quicker than arguing.

Step 2: Send the first reminder within three days

Don't wait two weeks to be polite. The longer an invoice sits, the further down the customer's list it falls. Keep the first message light and factual:

Hi Dana, a quick note that invoice 2026-031 for $3,200.00 was due on November 17. I've attached a copy. You can pay by the QR code on the invoice or by bank transfer. If anything about it is unclear, let me know and I'll sort it out today.

Four details do the work here: the invoice number, the exact amount, the actual due date, and a payment method. Leave out any hint of blame. Email subject lines that get opened are specific: "Invoice 2026-031, $3,200.00, was due Nov 17".

Step 3: Follow a schedule you don't have to think about

A fixed schedule takes the awkwardness out of chasing money. Here it is with real dates for a $3,200 invoice due November 17:

  • Nov 14 (3 days before): friendly heads-up with the payment link.
  • Nov 18 (1 day late): short reminder.
  • Nov 24 (7 days late): second reminder, invoice attached again.
  • Dec 1 (14 days late): phone call.
  • Dec 8 (21 days late): email recap of the call.
  • Dec 17 (30 days late): formal demand letter.
  • Jan 16 (60 days late): outside help.

Notice that no step is aggressive. Persistence means a contact every seven to ten days. Calling daily for a month is harassment, and in some places it breaks the law.

Step 4: Pick up the phone at day 14

An email is easy to ignore. A call isn't. Two things make the call work:

  1. Ask what's going on, then listen. "I'm calling about invoice 2026-031. Is there something holding it up on your side?" You'll learn whether it's a lost invoice, an approval delay, or a cash problem.
  2. Get a date, not a promise. "Can you pay it by Friday the 5th?" is better than "I'll try."

Right after the call, send a two-line email: "Thanks for your time today. As agreed, you'll pay $3,200.00 on or before Friday, December 5." That message is your record. If the date passes, you've got something concrete to point to.

If your contact keeps stalling, ask for the accounts or finance team. They can see the approval status and they care about process, not about you.

Step 5: Use late fees carefully, with real numbers

A late fee only works if the customer agreed to it before they were late. Put it in the quote, the contract and on the invoice. Adding one to an invoice already overdue invites an argument.

Three common structures, shown on the same $3,200 invoice that's 21 days late:

  • Flat fee: 5% of the balance is $160.00, charged once.
  • Monthly interest: 1.5% a month is $48.00 a month, about 18% a year. At 21 days that's roughly $33.60.
  • Fixed amount: $50 per month overdue, which is simple to explain.

Check your local rules first. Many US states cap late fees or interest for certain customers, and consumer jobs are often more tightly regulated than business-to-business ones. In the UK, businesses can claim statutory interest of 8% above the Bank of England base rate on late business debts, plus a fixed recovery fee of £40, £70 or £100 depending on the size of the debt. In the EU, the Late Payment Directive sets similar rights. If you're unsure, ask a local accountant or your small business office once and write the answer into your terms.

Also think about whether a fee is worth it. A $160 fee that turns a good customer into an enemy costs you more than it earns. For long-term clients, a polite reminder usually works better.

Step 6: Offer a payment plan before you escalate

When a customer can't pay all at once, a plan is better than nothing. Compare:

  • Full $3,200 in 90 days from a collections agency: agencies commonly keep 25% to 50% of what they recover. At 30% you'd receive about $2,240.
  • Four payments of $800 every two weeks: you receive the full $3,200 by mid-January.

Put the plan in writing, with each date and amount, and say that a missed payment makes the whole balance due. Take the first payment immediately if you can.

You can also trade a discount for speed. If they pay in full within 7 days you'll take 5% off ($160), so they pay $3,040. That's a worse deal than the full price, but better than a collections agency, and you have the money this week. If you plan to offer early-payment discounts on new invoices, "2/10 net 30" (2% off if paid within 10 days) costs you about 37% a year in annualised terms, so it's expensive. Use it only when cash flow really matters.

Step 7: Escalate in order

If the customer has gone quiet after a call and a recap, move up one rung at a time.

  1. Pause work. Give a week's notice: "I'll resume the project as soon as the balance is cleared." It protects you from doing more unpaid work.
  2. Formal demand letter (day 30). It should list the invoice number, the amount, the original due date, every earlier contact with its date, a final payment date (about 10 to 14 days away) and what you'll do if it isn't met. Send it by email and by tracked mail.
  3. Collections agency (day 60 or later). Most want to see documents: the signed quote, the invoice, the proof of delivery and your messages. Expect to pay a share of what they recover.
  4. Small claims court. Limits vary a lot. In the US they range from about $2,500 to $25,000 depending on the state, and in England the small claims track covers claims up to £10,000. Filing fees are often between $30 and $100 or more. You usually don't need a lawyer. A tidy folder with the quote, invoice, messages and photos of the finished work is what wins these.

Before you file, ask whether the amount justifies the time. For a $300 invoice, a firm demand letter often settles it. For $8,000, it's worth getting advice. If the customer files for bankruptcy, stop contacting them about the debt and speak to a professional about filing a claim.

Stop it happening again

The cheapest collection is the one you never need. After a late payer, change one of these things:

  • Take a deposit. 30% to 50% up front means a no-show only costs you the rest. See how to invoice a client for services for deposit and staged-payment examples.
  • Shorten the terms. Net 7 or net 14 instead of net 30 for small clients.
  • Invoice the same day the work is finished.
  • Make paying effortless. A pay link or QR code with the amount filled in beats typing bank details.
  • Write the late-fee and pause-work terms into every quote.
  • Review unpaid invoices weekly. Group them as 1 to 30, 31 to 60 and 61+ days late, and call anyone in the last group this week.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I wait before following up on a late payment?

One to three days after the due date. Earlier is better, because the invoice is still near the top of their inbox, and a friendly reminder at this stage rarely upsets anyone.

Can I charge a late fee if it isn't in the contract?

Usually it's a bad idea and sometimes it isn't allowed. A fee the customer never agreed to is easy to dispute. Add the terms to your quote and invoice from now on, and check your local rules.

What should I do when a customer ignores every message?

Call, then try a different contact such as accounts payable, then send a formal demand letter with a final date. If that fails, decide between a collections agency and small claims court based on the amount.
