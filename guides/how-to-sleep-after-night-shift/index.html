How to Sleep After Night Shift: 9 Fixes and a Worked Day

If you want to know how to sleep after night shift, the short answer is this: go to bed soon after you get home, make the room properly dark, mask daytime noise with a steady sound, and stop caffeine at least 4 hours before your shift ends. Your body clock is telling you it's morning, so the job is to give it as few "it's daytime" signals as possible. Everything below is a way to do that.

Most guidance says aim for 7 to 9 hours of sleep in every 24, but night workers usually get less and sleep worse than day workers. Even 6 hours or fewer, night after night, adds up to sleep debt. The nine fixes below are ranked roughly by how much difference they make for most people.

The quick answer

  • On the way home: wear sunglasses (only if someone else is driving) and skip bright screens.
  • At home: eat something light, then go straight to bed within about 30 to 60 minutes.
  • In the bedroom: blackout curtains or an eye mask, a cool room around 60 to 67°F (16 to 19°C), phone on Do Not Disturb, steady background sound.
  • Before your shift: a 90-minute nap in the afternoon, or at least 20 to 30 minutes.
  • If you're stuck: accept that any sleep beats none, and tell your doctor if it lasts for weeks.

One honest note first: the evidence for shift work sleep advice is mostly low quality. A BMJ summary from two doctors says so directly and offers its plan as a starting point. So treat the list as experiments, and keep what works for your body.

1. Go to bed soon after you get home

The longer you stay up after the sun rises, the more awake your body clock makes you feel. Sleepiness from the night is real, but it fades fast once morning light and breakfast-time noise hit. Occupational health guides for nurses and the military both say the same thing: go straight to bed.

Worked example: your shift ends at 7am and you're home by 7:45. Shower, a small snack, and be in bed by 8:30. If you stay up to do chores until 10, you may find the sleepy window has closed and you lie there wide awake at 10:15 with only 5 hours before the afternoon.

Some people do better with a split schedule, such as a short sleep right after work and another before the next shift. Public health guidance lists both as reasonable. Try each for a week and see.

2. Protect your eyes from morning light

Light on your eyes suppresses melatonin, the hormone that helps you feel sleepy. A bright commute is a big "wake up" signal.

  • Wear sunglasses on the way home, even if it's cloudy. If you drive, don't wear them when you're drowsy, because seeing less when you're tired is risky. Public transport or a lift home is safer after a long night.
  • Turn on your phone's night mode during the last 2 hours of the shift, and dim the lights if your job allows it.
  • Keep screens out of the last 30 to 60 minutes before bed.

3. Make the bedroom truly dark

Daylight is the main reason day sleep is lighter. Aim for a room so dark you can't see your hand in front of your face.

  1. Blackout curtains or blinds over every window, with the edges taped or clipped, since light creeps in around the sides.
  2. An eye mask as a backup. Choose one that doesn't press on your eyelids.
  3. Block the gap under the door, and cover standby lights with small stickers.
  4. A dim red nightlight if you need to get up. Red light disturbs your body clock less than white.

Cost check: blackout curtains run roughly $25 to $60 a window, and a good eye mask is under $15. The mask is the best first step.

4. Mask the daytime noise

At 10am the world is loud. Lawn mowers, delivery trucks, school drop-off, a neighbour's drill. Sudden sounds are what jolt you awake from a light daytime sleep, because your body clock already thinks it should be awake.

A steady sound helps because it makes sudden noises less obvious. That's why the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's shift work guide suggests a fan, a white noise machine or a white noise phone app. Which to pick:

  • For traffic and engines: brown noise or a box fan. Deeper sound covers low rumble.
  • For voices, kids and TV through the wall: white or pink noise. Higher frequencies cover speech.
  • If noise feels harsh at 9am: steady rain or ocean waves.

Keep the volume well under the level of normal conversation and put the speaker across the room rather than under your pillow. Earplugs work too, and some people use both.

Evidence for continuous noise is mixed, so test it for a week and judge for yourself.

5. Stop caffeine 4 hours before the shift ends

Coffee at 5am for a 7am finish is still in your system when you lie down. Clinicians suggest stopping at least 4 hours before the end of your shift. The BMJ summary adds a neat trick: have your last caffeine just before a short nap, and make that your final cup of the night.

Worked example (7pm to 7am shift):

  • 7:30pm: first coffee, fine.
  • 1am: second coffee before a 20-minute break nap.
  • 3am: switch to water or decaf.
  • 7am: nothing in your system that fights sleep.

Alcohol is a bad shortcut. A nightcap may help you nod off, but it breaks up the second half of your sleep.

6. Eat light, not hungry and not stuffed

Going to bed hungry wakes people up. A heavy fatty meal is hard to digest and does the same. NIOSH suggests a small meal with protein and carbohydrate, like cereal with milk, if you'll be hungry. During the night, choose light snacks and water over a huge 3am meal.

7. Use naps on purpose

Naps are one of the best-supported tools for night workers.

  • Before your first night shift: a 90-minute nap between about 2pm and 6pm completes one full sleep cycle. If you only have time for a short one, 20 to 30 minutes still helps without much grogginess.
  • During the shift (if allowed): 10 to 20 minutes early in the night. Longer naps risk waking in deep sleep.
  • Before driving home: keep it to 10 to 15 minutes.
  • Avoid: an hour or more of nap close to the end of your shift. It steals from your main sleep.

8. Ask the people around you for help

You can't fix a loud house with a better pillow. Practical asks that cost nothing:

  1. Tell family or roommates your sleep hours, for example "8:30am to 4:30pm".
  2. Ask for no vacuuming, dishwasher or loud TV in that window.
  3. Put a "Do Not Disturb" note on the front door for deliveries.
  4. Silence calls and texts with Do Not Disturb, and allow only emergency contacts through.
  5. If you rent, a polite word with neighbours can help.

9. Pick one schedule and keep it

A steady pattern beats a perfect one. The Cleveland Clinic advises keeping a regular sleep time even on days off, if you can, so your body can adapt. For many people that's hard when family life runs on days, so use one of these:

  • Anchor sleep: on days off, still sleep at least 4 to 5 hours at your night-shift time, then add a nap in the afternoon.
  • Full flip: go back to a day schedule on your days off. The BMJ summary suggests a 90 or 180-minute nap straight after your last shift, going outside when you wake, then bed close to your normal time that evening.

Whichever you pick, try it for 2 to 3 weeks before deciding it doesn't work.

A sample day for a 7pm to 7am shift

  1. 7:00am: shift ends. Sunglasses on if you're not driving.
  2. 7:45am: home. Light snack, shower, phone on Do Not Disturb.
  3. 8:30am: in bed, mask on, steady sound on a timer to stop at 3:30pm.
  4. 3:30pm: wake (7 hours). Get daylight for 10 minutes, eat a proper meal.
  5. 4pm to 6pm: errands, family, exercise. Finish hard exercise at least 2 hours before your next sleep.
  6. 6:30pm: small snack, head to work.

When to talk to a doctor

See a doctor if you still can't fall or stay asleep after several weeks, if you feel tired after 7 to 8 hours, if you nod off while driving, or if you snore loudly or stop breathing in your sleep. You might have shift work sleep disorder, sleep apnea or something else that's very treatable. Don't self-medicate with over-the-counter sleep aids on a work schedule, since many leave you drowsy for hours. This guide is general information, not medical advice.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I sleep after a night shift?

Aim for 7 to 9 hours in each 24-hour period. If you can't get it in one block, split it: for example 5 hours after work and a 90-minute nap before the next shift. Any sleep is better than none.

Should I sleep right after a night shift or stay up?

Most guidance says go to bed soon after you get home, because staying up makes you more awake as the sun climbs. Some people prefer a short sleep after work and a longer one in the afternoon. Test both for a week.

Is white noise good for sleeping during the day?

It can help, mainly by covering sudden sounds like traffic and voices. Evidence is mixed, so keep the volume low, use a timer and see if you wake less often. Brown noise and a box fan suit low rumble. White or pink noise suits voices.
