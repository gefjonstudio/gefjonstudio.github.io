How to Order Food in Spanish: What to Say, and What They'll Say Back

To order food in Spanish, greet the waiter, then use one of three patterns: "Me gustaría..." (I'd like...), "Para mí..." (for me...) or "¿Me trae...?" (could you bring me...?), and finish with "por favor". When you're done, ask for the bill with "La cuenta, por favor." That's enough to eat well in any Spanish-speaking country.

Most phrase lists stop there, and then the waiter says something you didn't practise. This guide covers what you say, what you'll hear back, and the words that change between Spain, Mexico and 3 other countries.

Quick answer

  1. Say "Buenas tardes" (good afternoon) or "Buenas noches" when you sit down.
  2. Ask for the menu: "La carta, por favor" in Spain, "El menú, por favor" in Mexico.
  3. Order with "Para mí, [dish], por favor" or "Me gustaría [dish]."
  4. Mention allergies before you order, not after.
  5. Ask for the bill yourself: "La cuenta, por favor." In Spain, it often won't arrive until you do.

The 3 sentence patterns that do most of the work

1. "Me gustaría..." or "Quisiera..." Both mean "I'd like..." and both are polite everywhere. "Me gustaría una ensalada mixta, por favor." (I'd like a mixed salad, please.)

2. "Para mí..." Literally "for me". It's what many locals say, it's short, and it works for every dish. "Para mí, el pollo asado." (The roast chicken for me.) For someone else, swap in "para él" (for him) or "para ella" (for her).

3. "¿Me trae...?" or "¿Me podría traer...?" Could you bring me... "¿Me podría traer más pan, por favor?" (Could you bring me more bread, please?)

You can say "Quiero..." (I want...). It isn't rude, but it's blunt. "Me da..." (give me...) is common in Spain at a bar and sounds fine from a local. If you're not sure, use "Me gustaría".

The verb for "to order" is pedir. Latin America also uses ordenar, but in Spain it usually means to tidy up.

Extra lines worth knowing

  • Table and drinks: "Una mesa para dos, por favor." For 4 people, "Somos cuatro." Then "Para beber, una cerveza y un agua sin gas."
  • Not sure what to pick: "¿Qué me recomienda?" (What do you recommend?) It's the most useful question you can ask.
  • How many: "Dos cafés" (2 coffees), "tres tacos" (3 tacos), "cuatro cervezas" (4 beers), "una mesa para seis" (a table for 6), "ocho tapas" (8 tapas).
  • What's in it: "¿Qué lleva?" or "¿Lleva carne?" (Does it have meat?)
  • Changes: "Sin cebolla, por favor." (No onion.) "Sin picante." (Not spicy.)
  • The bill: "La cuenta, por favor." To split it: "¿Podemos pagar por separado?" To check cards work: "¿Aceptan tarjeta?"

What the waiter will say back

This is the part phrase lists skip. Expect these:

  • "¿Están listos para pedir?" Are you ready to order? Say "Un momento, por favor" if you need time.
  • "¿Qué van a tomar?" What will you have? Answer with "Para mí..."
  • "¿Cómo quiere la carne?" How would you like the meat? Answers: "poco hecha" (rare), "al punto" (medium), "muy hecha" (well done).
  • "¿Algo más?" Anything else? Say "Nada más, gracias." (Nothing else, thanks.)

Spain and Mexico: the same language, different menus

Spanish menus change by country, and even by city. These are the differences that trip travellers up most.

  • Waiter: camarero in Spain, mesero in Mexico. You can also just say "Perdone" or "Disculpe" and catch their eye.
  • Orange juice: zumo in Spain, jugo in Latin America.
  • Potatoes: patatas in Spain, papas in Latin America.
  • Prawns: gambas in Spain, camarones in Mexico.
  • Peanuts: cacahuetes in Spain, cacahuates in Mexico, maní in Argentina, Chile and much of South America. This one matters for allergies.
  • Tortilla: in Spain it's a thick potato omelette (tortilla de patatas). In Mexico it's a flatbread made of corn or wheat. Order one in the wrong country and you get a very different plate.
  • Tipping: Mexico 10 to 15 percent, usually not on the bill. Spain 0 to 5 percent, often just rounding up. Ask "¿Está incluido el servicio?" (Is service included?) if unsure.
  • Timing: in Spain lunch is often around 2pm to 4pm and dinner starts late, often after 8:30pm. Many places serve a menú del día, a fixed-price weekday lunch of 2 courses, dessert or coffee and 1 drink, often for roughly 12 € to 16 €.

Six worked examples you can copy

1. Menú del día at lunch

Waiter: "¿Qué van a tomar de primero?" You: "De primero, la sopa de verduras. De segundo, el pollo asado. Y para beber, agua con gas." (Vegetable soup first, roast chicken second, sparkling water.) Then: "De postre, flan." The set price covers all of it, so you don't order dessert and coffee separately unless the menu says so.

2. Sharing at a tapas bar

Plates come in three sizes: tapa (small), media ración (half) and ración (full). "Vamos a compartir. Una ración de croquetas y media de ensaladilla." (We're going to share. A full portion of croquettes and a half of Russian salad.) "Vamos a compartir" tells the waiter to bring everything to the middle of the table.

3. Tacos in Mexico City

At a taco stand you may order standing up. "Voy a querer tres tacos al pastor, por favor." (I'll have three al pastor tacos.) The taquero asks "¿Con todo?" (With everything, meaning onion and cilantro). Say "Con todo" or "Sin cebolla". Spice question: "¿Pica mucho?" (Is it very hot?). Pay with "¿Cuánto es?" (How much?).

4. An allergy order

Say it first, slowly, before the menu questions: "Soy alérgico a los cacahuetes. ¿Este plato los lleva?" (I'm allergic to peanuts. Does this dish have them?) Women say alérgica. Other useful lines: "Soy celíaco" (I have coeliac disease), "No como cerdo" (I don't eat pork), "Soy alérgico al marisco" (I'm allergic to shellfish). For vegetarians: "Soy vegetariano. ¿Tiene algo sin carne ni pescado?" Be careful here: a "vegetarian" dish can still be made with ham or meat stock, so ask "¿Lleva jamón o caldo de carne?" In Spain and the rest of the EU, restaurants must be able to tell you which of the 14 major allergens (peanuts, gluten, shellfish, eggs, milk and others) are in each dish, so ask "¿Tiene la carta de alérgenos?" (Do you have the allergen list?). For a serious allergy, a written note in Spanish is safer than speaking. Don't rely on one phrase alone.

5. Reading a menú del día and working out the bill

Here's an example board, with made-up prices in the typical range. "Menú del día, 14,50 €. Primeros: lentejas, ensalada mixta, sopa de pescado. Segundos: merluza a la plancha, pollo al ajillo, filete de ternera. Postre o café. Bebida incluida." That's 3 first courses, 3 main courses and 1 price. You say: "El menú del día, por favor. De primero, las lentejas, y de segundo, la merluza." For 2 people that's 29 €, and in Spain nobody expects a big extra on top. If you pay 38,40 € at a tapas bar, rounding up to 40 € is a friendly gesture.

In Mexico City a 480 peso dinner bill means a 10 percent tip is 48 pesos and 15 percent is 72 pesos, so 50 to 70 pesos is reasonable. In Buenos Aires, a 25.000 peso bill means about 2.500 pesos on top.

6. A sample menu to practise on

Read the dish, then say your order out loud. These prices are made up, in the typical range for a casual place in Spain:

  • Tortilla de patatas (potato omelette), 6,50 €
  • Croquetas de jamón (ham croquettes), 8,00 €
  • Patatas bravas (spicy potatoes), 5,50 €
  • Pimientos de Padrón (small fried green peppers), 7,00 €
  • Pulpo a la gallega (octopus with paprika), 16,00 €
  • Gambas al ajillo (garlic prawns), 12,50 €
  • Paella para dos (paella for two), 28,00 €
  • Flan casero (home-made custard), 4,00 €

Your order for 2 people: "Para empezar, una tortilla y unas patatas bravas. Después, la paella para dos. Y de postre, un flan para compartir." That's 6,50 + 5,50 + 28,00 + 4,00, so 44,00 € before drinks.

Beyond Spain and Mexico

Spanish is the main language in about 20 countries, and the menu words keep shifting. A few quick notes, with the usual tip customs (always check the bill, because rules change):

  • Argentina (Buenos Aires): the waiter is a mozo, and locals say "¿Qué querés?" instead of "¿Qué quieres?". Around 10 percent is the usual tip.
  • Chile: avocado is palta, and bills often show a suggested 10 percent tip you can accept or change.
  • Colombia: tinto means a small black coffee. Many bills add a voluntary 10 percent service charge, which you can ask to remove.
  • Costa Rica: a casado is the set plate of the day. Bills commonly add 13 percent tax and 10 percent service, so the 23 percent you see is normal.
  • Peru: set lunches called menús are common and cheap, with 2 or 3 courses.

When your Spanish runs out

Everyone freezes at some point. Regional dishes (pulpo a feira, cochinita pibil) aren't in any phrase list, and a chalkboard menu is hard to read at speed. Frequently asked questions

How do you politely order food in Spanish?

Greet the waiter, then say "Me gustaría..." or "Para mí..." plus the dish, and add "por favor".

How do you ask for the bill in Spanish?

Say "La cuenta, por favor." To split it, ask "¿Podemos pagar por separado?"
