Learning how to measure card centering is the cheapest way to avoid wasting a grading fee. Centering is the one flaw you can check accurately at home, and it caps the grade: a card with perfect corners still can't get a PSA 10 if it's cut too far to one side.

This guide shows the formula, three ways to measure (ruler, photo, app), the published limits for the main graders, and six worked examples with real numbers.

What card centering means

Centering compares the border on one side of the card with the border on the opposite side. It's written as two numbers that add up to 100, such as 55/45: the wider border is 55% of the two borders added together.

  • 50/50 is perfect.
  • 60/40 means one border is noticeably wider.
  • 75/25 is badly off-center.

Every card has two directions (left/right and top/bottom) and two faces (front and back). Graders look at all four, and the worst number on each face is the one that counts.

The formula

For each pair of opposite borders:

Centering = wider border ÷ (left border + right border) × 100

Then write it as that number over the rest of 100. Example: left border 2.4 mm, right border 1.6 mm. Total is 4.0 mm. 2.4 ÷ 4.0 = 0.60, so the card is 60/40 left to right.

Any unit works (millimetres, inches, pixels) because only the ratio matters.

Three ways to measure

1. With a ruler or calipers

  1. Take the card out of the sleeve or toploader; plastic hides the true edge.
  2. Measure from the cut edge to where the border colour stops (the inner frame), across the middle of each side.
  3. Measure both ends of each border too. If the card was cut on a tilt, use the wider reading, because graders measure the worst point.
  4. Do left/right, then top/bottom, then flip the card and repeat for the back.

A clear millimetre ruler works, but borders are often only 2 to 3 mm wide, so half a millimetre of error can move the result by about 10 points. Digital calipers are much better.

2. From a photo or scan

Put the card flat, shoot straight down so the card fills the frame, then zoom in and count pixels across each border. A flatbed scan at 600 dpi is even better: one pixel is about 0.04 mm. The photo must be square to the card, because a tilted camera makes the near border look wider.

Centering limits by grading company

These are the front and back limits for the top grades, based on each company's published standards. "Approx." is the companies' own wording; always check the current standards before you submit.

PSA

  • PSA 10 Gem Mint: approx. 55/45 front, 75/25 back
  • PSA 9 Mint: approx. 60/40 front, 90/10 back
  • PSA 8 NM-MT: approx. 65/35 front, 90/10 back

BGS (Beckett)

  • Pristine 10: 50/50 front, 55/45 back
  • Gem Mint 9.5: 55/45 both ways front, 60/40 back
  • Mint 9: 55/45 both ways front, 70/30 back

CGC

  • Gem Mint 10: approx. 55/45 front, 75/25 back
  • Mint 9: 60/40 front, 90/10 back

SGC

  • SGC 10: approx. 55/45 front. SGC doesn't publish a separate back figure.

The pattern to remember: for a 10 at PSA, CGC or SGC, aim for 55/45 or better on the front. Beckett is stricter on both faces.

6 worked examples

Example 1: The near miss

Front left 2.3 mm, right 1.8 mm. Total 4.1 mm. 2.3 ÷ 4.1 = 56.1%, so 56/44. Top/bottom is 52/48. The worse axis is 56/44, which is just outside 55/45. This card is likely capped at a PSA 9 on centering, even if everything else is perfect.

Example 2: Great front, bad back

Front 52/48 both ways. Back left 3.1 mm, right 0.9 mm: 3.1 ÷ 4.0 = 77.5%, so 78/22. The back is outside PSA's 75/25 limit, so the back caps this card at a 9 even though the front is excellent. Always measure the back.

Example 3: The tilted cut

Left border is 2.0 mm at the top and 2.6 mm at the bottom; right border is 2.0 mm at the top and 1.4 mm at the bottom. At the middle it looks fine, but at the bottom the ratio is 2.6 ÷ 4.0 = 65/35. Graders use the worst point, so this card measures 65/35, not the 50/50 you see in the middle.

Example 4: Top and bottom matter too

Left/right is a perfect 50/50. Top 3.6 mm, bottom 2.4 mm: 3.6 ÷ 6.0 = 60/40. It looks centered at a glance because people only check the sides, but it's a PSA 9 on centering.

Example 5: Same card, different graders

Front 55/45 both ways, back 62/38. That fits PSA 10 (55/45 front, 75/25 back) and CGC Gem Mint 10, but not BGS 9.5, whose back limit is 60/40. If you want a Beckett label, this card is a weaker candidate.

Example 6: Counting pixels

On a 600 dpi scan, the left border is 58 pixels and the right is 44 pixels. 58 ÷ 102 = 56.9%, so 57/43. Re-scan with the card rotated 180 degrees: you get 56/44. Take the worse reading (57/43). Measuring twice with the card turned removes small scanner errors.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring in the sleeve. The plastic edge isn't the card edge.
  • Only checking left and right. Top/bottom off-centering is just as common.
  • Ignoring the back. It has looser limits, but it still caps the grade.
  • Photos taken at an angle. The border closer to the camera looks bigger.
  • Using the wrong inner line. Measure to where the border colour ends (the frame), not to the artwork inside it, unless the card has no frame.
  • Rounding in your favour. 55.6/44.4 isn't 55/45.

Is my card worth grading?

Check centering first because it's free and decisive. If the front is worse than 60/40, a PSA 10 is off the table no matter how clean the corners are. If centering passes, check corners, edges and surface under a bright light. Only then compare the expected value of the graded card against the grading fee and shipping.

Frequently asked questions

What centering do you need for a PSA 10?

Approximately 55/45 or better on the front and 75/25 or better on the back, based on PSA's published grading standards.

Does back centering matter?

Yes. Every major grader checks the back. The limits are looser (75/25 for a PSA 10), but a badly off-center back can still cap the grade.

Can you fix bad centering?

No. Centering comes from how the card was cut at the factory. Trimming a card to "fix" it's detected by graders and the card won't be graded.

How accurate are centering apps?

Good apps straighten the photo and let you place the lines on the borders yourself with a magnifier, which is as accurate as a scan. Treat any predicted grade as an estimate: only the grading company decides the final grade.
